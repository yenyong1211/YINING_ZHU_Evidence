## P1 — Detailed Think-Aloud

Prototype A — Facts-based Label

P1 spent more attention on the specific privacy facts rather than trying to form an overall privacy judgement immediately.

The participant particularly focused on Manufacturer Access. Their concern was not simply whether the manufacturer can access recordings, but:

Does the manufacturer need my permission before accessing my recordings?

This is important because P1 appeared to interpret permission/consent as the key boundary between acceptable and unacceptable manufacturer access. The participant later repeatedly emphasised that manufacturer access should make the permission condition prominent.

P1 was also able to use the facts-based layout to look for individual pieces of information. This became important later when comparing A/B/C: although A was not necessarily the most visually expressive design, the participant felt it was useful because they could directly inspect the privacy facts themselves.

P1's thinking during A can therefore be summarised as:

“Tell me exactly what happens, and let me judge it myself.”

P1's suggestion for A

The participant's feedback suggests that Manufacturer Access should not simply say:

Manufacturer Access — Conditional

Instead, it should explain the condition:

Manufacturer AccessMay access recordings for technical support.User permission required.

Or, where the permission requirement cannot be verified:

User permission: Not clearly specified

So P1 was effectively asking for more specific conditions, not simply more information.

## P1 — Prototype B Think-Aloud

Prototype B introduced a numerical summary. P1 could see that the numbers were intended to summarise privacy characteristics, but this created a new question that Prototype A did not create:

“Where does this rating come from?”

This became one of P1's strongest comments.

The participant did not simply accept the number as an objective measurement. Instead, they started questioning:

Who calculates the rating?

Is it calculated by the manufacturer?

Could a manufacturer give itself a better score?

What information is used to produce the number?

Why should the user trust this number?

P1 therefore moved from asking:

“What happens to my data?”

in Prototype A to asking:

“Why should I trust your interpretation of what happens to my data?”

That is a really useful testing result.

P1 also felt that the score occupied a relatively large amount of visual space. Their comments suggest that the score appeared more important than the evidence behind it, even though the participant personally cared more about the actual privacy facts.

P1's suggestion for B

If a rating is retained, P1's feedback suggests it should include:

How is this calculated?

and allow users to see the underlying factors.

For example:

Data Protection: 6/8 — High

Based on:

Manufacturer Access

Cloud Storage

Third-party Sharing

Encryption

View calculation →

The score should therefore act as a summary of evidence, not replace the evidence.

## P1 — Prototype C Think-Aloud

P1 found the visual representation easier for understanding the CCTV's overall position once the two dimensions were understood.

However, there was an important problem with the original axes:

Data Exposure: higher = worseUser Control: higher = better

The participant needed additional explanation to understand that the two dimensions moved in opposite evaluative directions.

So although C made the overall relationship more visual, the visualisation itself did not automatically solve the interpretation problem.

P1's reaction suggests:

Visualisation can make the overall privacy profile easier to see, but only if the dimensions themselves are immediately understandable.

P1's suggestion for C

Use dimensions with the same direction, for example:

Data Protection ↑User Control ↑

Then:

Higher = Better

This would make the top-right position immediately understandable without requiring users to remember that one dimension is positive while the other is negative.

P1 — Researcher Notes

P1 did not reject visual summaries completely. Prototype C helped with overall understanding, but the participant ultimately preferred A because it gave more direct access to the actual privacy facts.

The key tension identified by P1 is therefore: Summary vs. Evidence

A score or visualisation can make privacy information faster to interpret, but adding interpretation also creates a new requirement: users need to understand and trust how that interpretation was produced.

Prototype A — Facts-based Label

P2's attention initially moved across several categories, including:

This suggests the participant was not reading the label simply from top to bottom. They were scanning for privacy concepts that seemed important.

When asked who could access the recordings, P2 initially focused on manufacturer access, but then corrected the answer to:

The participant also successfully located cloud storage information.

So the problem was not that P2 could not find anything. The participant could find concrete information reasonably well.

The problem appeared when the information became conditional or vague.

P2 — “For specific purposes”

This is probably P2's most useful think-aloud moment.

When P2 saw the Data Sharing / Manufacturer Access wording, they stopped and asked:

“What does ‘for specific purposes’ mean?”

That tells you something more specific than “P2 was confused.”

P2 was effectively asking:

What purpose?Technical support?Security?System maintenance?Advertising?Legal request?

The phrase technically gives the user information, but does not give enough information for the user to judge whether the access is acceptable.

P2's suggestion for A

Avoid: For specific purposes

Instead: Purpose: Technical support

or: Purpose: Technical support and service operation

And ideally also show: Permission required: Yes / No / Unknown

So P2's feedback suggests that privacy information needs to explain not only: Who?

but also: Why? + When? + With whose permission?

## P2 — Prototype B Think-Aloud

When P2 saw B, the participant recognised that the new elements were ratings/scores and tried to interpret their meaning.

P2 asked whether the rating meant: “the risk is quite high?”

This is important because it shows that P2 was using the score to form a quick overall privacy judgement.

Unlike P1, P2 did not strongly challenge the credibility of the score in the available transcript. Instead, the main issue was interpretation:

What exactly is this number telling me?

After clarification, P2 indicated that the risk level and User Control level were understandable.

So for P2: Rating = potentially useful shortcut, but requires clear meaning.

This is slightly different from P1: P1 = Can I trust the rating?P2 = What does the rating mean?

That difference is worth preserving rather than merging both participants into one identical finding.

P2 — Manufacturer Access + Encryption

P2 also showed uncertainty when answering whether the manufacturer could access encrypted recordings.

The participant interpreted this as: “It depends on my settings, right?”

and connected manufacturer access with the user's encryption settings.

This suggests that the prototype did not clearly separate:

Encryption

from

Access permission

from

Manufacturer access capability

For an ordinary user, these can easily become one idea:

“If it is encrypted, maybe the manufacturer cannot access it.”

But those are technically different privacy concepts.

P2's suggestion implied by this behaviour

The label should avoid forcing users to infer the relationship.

For example:

Manufacturer AccessTechnical support access may be possible.Permission: RequiredEncrypted recordings: [verified condition]

rather than scattering these facts across different sections.

## P2 Test Summary

Participant 2 (P2) was able to identify several key privacy facts from the first prototype. The participant correctly recognised that the CCTV owner and authorised users could access the recordings, identified cloud storage, and found several available controls, including retention settings, camera and microphone controls, and encryption.

However, P2 showed uncertainty when interpreting Manufacturer Access and Data Sharing. In particular, the phrase “for specific purposes” was unclear because it did not explain what those purposes were or under what conditions manufacturer access or data sharing could occur. P2 rated their confidence in understanding the privacy information as 3/5.

When viewing the rating-based prototype, P2 understood that the numerical information represented privacy-related risk and user control, but initially needed clarification about what the scores represented. After explanation, the participant said that the risk and control levels were understandable.

P2 also interpreted manufacturer access to encrypted recordings as depending on the user's encryption settings, suggesting that the relationship between encryption, manufacturer access, and user control needs to be communicated more clearly.

## P1 + P2 Combined Summary

The first two tests suggest that participants can generally find and understand concrete privacy facts, such as who can access recordings, where recordings are stored, and what controls are available. However, more abstract or conditional information creates greater uncertainty.

Both participants showed some difficulty understanding manufacturer access, particularly the conditions under which a manufacturer may access recordings. P2 specifically questioned what “for specific purposes” meant, while P1 strongly emphasised that manufacturer access should clearly explain whether user permission is required.

The tests also identified problems with the numerical rating approach. P1 questioned where the scores came from and whether they could be trusted, while P2 required some explanation before understanding what the risk and user-control scores represented.

Overall, the early testing suggests that the Privacy Label is more useful when it prioritises clear privacy facts first, while ratings should act only as a secondary summary and should explain how they were calculated.

Emerging Findings from P1 + P2

TF1 — Concrete privacy facts are easier to understand than abstract ratings.

Both participants were able to locate information about storage, access and privacy controls. However, numerical ratings required additional interpretation. This suggests that specific privacy facts should remain the primary information in the label, while scores should only provide a quick summary.

Design implication:Keep Manufacturer Access, Storage, Data Sharing, Retention and User Control visible. Do not allow the score to replace these facts.

TF2 — Manufacturer access conditions need clearer explanation.

Both participants showed uncertainty about when manufacturers could access CCTV recordings. P2 specifically found “for specific purposes” unclear, while P1 wanted manufacturer access to clearly communicate whether permission was required.

Design implication:

Instead of:

Manufacturer AccessFor specific purposes

use something more concrete, for example:

Manufacturer AccessAccess may occur for technical support.User permission is required.

And if the real product information does not confirm that permission is required, do not invent it—show:

Permission requirement: Not clearly specified

TF3 — The meaning and credibility of ratings are not immediately clear.

P1 questioned where the score came from and whether it could be manipulated, while P2 needed clarification about what the risk and control ratings represented. This suggests that presenting a score without its calculation method may reduce rather than increase transparency.

Design implication:

Add:

How is this score calculated?

and allow users to see the factors behind the score.

This also supports your idea from earlier of changing the two dimensions so they use the same direction, for example:

Data Protection: 6/8User Control: 7/8Higher = Better

TF4 — Technical and conditional wording can reduce confidence.

P2's confidence was only 3/5, despite being able to answer several factual questions correctly. The participant was particularly uncertain around encryption, manufacturer access and conditional language.

Design implication:Use simpler language and explain conditions directly rather than relying on terms such as “specific purposes,” “optional access,” or technical encryption wording.
